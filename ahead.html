Ahead

What is coming, and where.

The other half of the watch. That page records what already happened; this one lists what has a date on it, so there is still time to do something about it. Soonest first. Every line carries a date, a place and a source.

How to read this

A door is different from a diary entry.

Most of what follows is a meeting somebody else is holding. You can watch it, read what comes out of it, and hold it to what it said. That is worth knowing, but it is not participation.

A few entries are marked Open door. Those are consultations, calls for evidence and comment periods: a published process where an ordinary person or a small organisation can put something in writing and have it counted. Those are the ones that matter most here, because Q9 asks who the many are and how they are actually asked, and a door nobody walked through is part of that answer.

Dates are given as the source states them. Where a source says a month but not a day, the entry says so rather than inventing precision. Defence and military events are left out under the same standing rule as the watch.

EU AI Act transparency rules start to apply

European Union, all member states

From this date a provider must tell people when they are dealing with an AI system, and mark synthetic audio, images, video and text so that a machine can read the marking. Systems already on the market have until 2 December 2026 to carry it. The rules for high-risk systems sat on this same date until the end of July: the Digital Omnibus on AI entered into force on 27 July 2026 and moved the Annex III list, which covers employment, education, critical infrastructure and law enforcement, to 2 December 2027, and high-risk systems built into products already covered by EU safety law to 2 August 2028.

Scanned 21:44 Brisbane, 31 July 2026

A date this page had set for 2 August has moved to December 2027.

The line for the EU AI Act said that the remainder of the Act took effect on 2 August 2026. That was the law until 27 July, when the Digital Omnibus on AI entered into force and moved the obligations for high-risk systems out by sixteen months, to 2 December 2027, and for high-risk systems inside regulated products to 2 August 2028. The transparency rules still start on 2 August. The entry has been rewritten and the old wording is kept in the record. The source it relied on, a well-known implementation timeline, was last updated in August 2024, which is the lesson worth taking: a page that tracks dates has to be re-checked against the law and not against a summary of it.

Three doors are still open, and on 28 July this page said there were none. That entry was wrong, and it is corrected here rather than quietly removed. Two of the three were already open when it was written: the Australian Senate inquiry into AI and data centres, which had been taking submissions since May, and the Canadian consultation on AI transparency, which opened on 23 July. The third, the call for session proposals for the December Internet Governance Forum, closes today.

The search that missed them looked for consultations run by AI regulators. Two of these three are not: one is a parliamentary committee and one is an industry department. That is worth saying because it is the general case. A published process where an ordinary person can put something in writing and have it counted is more often held by a committee, a department or a meeting secretariat than by anything with AI in its name.

Corrections are still welcome. Open doors are announced quietly and close quickly, and the record of what this page got wrong is kept in the same file as what it got right.

Entries move to what already came once the date passes. They are kept there with a note of what came of it where the watch logged an outcome, so an announcement can be checked against what actually happened. Every entry is also held one per line in a machine-readable file.
