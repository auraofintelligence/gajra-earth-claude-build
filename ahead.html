What is coming, and where.

The other half of the watch. That page records what already happened; this one lists what has a date on it, so there is still time to do something about it. Soonest first. Every line carries a date, a place and a source.

How to read this

A door is different from a diary entry.

Most of what follows is a meeting somebody else is holding. You can watch it, read what comes out of it, and hold it to what it said. That is worth knowing, but it is not participation.

A few entries are marked Open door. Those are consultations, calls for evidence and comment periods: a published process where an ordinary person or a small organisation can put something in writing and have it counted. Those are the ones that matter most here, because Q9 asks who the many are and how they are actually asked, and a door nobody walked through is part of that answer.

Dates are given as the source states them. Where a source says a month but not a day, the entry says so rather than inventing precision. Defence and military events are left out under the same standing rule as the watch.

Ofgem opened a consultation on 29 July on reforming the process by which large electricity users connect to the grid. The problem it names is the queue itself. South Africa is to reopen its national AI policy for comment, after withdrawing the first draft Open door Q9

South Africa, written comment

The Department of Communications and Digital Technologies published a draft national artificial intelligence policy on 10 April 2026 and gave the public sixty days to comment on it. The draft was withdrawn on 12 June, because it cited sources that do not exist; the reporting puts that down to AI having been used in writing it without the citations being checked. The department said in May that a revised policy would go to Cabinet by November and that public comment would be invited from January 2027. That is why this is on the page with a month and no day: the month comes from the department's own statement, no notice has been published, and until one is there is nothing to write into. It is worth holding the whole sequence together rather than only the ending. A government put out a policy on artificial intelligence that artificial intelligence had partly invented, the public comment window ran over a document that could not be relied on, and the correction is a second window nine months later. Whether it opens in January is the first checkable thing; whether the sixty days come back with it is the second.

Scanned 07:34 Brisbane, 29 August 2026

Sixty-nine entries, forty-one of them doors, and the first proposal this page has carried that would sit under the sea.

Start at Eastport, a town of about 1,300 people at the eastern edge of Maine. DeepGreen Holdings, registered in Delaware a month before it applied to federal regulators, wants to put tidal-powered artificial intelligence data centres on the sea floor of Western Passage in the Bay of Fundy, across about twenty-seven acres, at a cost put at a hundred million dollars. Everything this page usually carries is an argument about land: which paddock, whose street, how far from the school. This one is in the water, and it has produced two dated things at once. Residents wrote two ordinances themselves rather than waiting for the council to write them, one to ban data centres and one to require a town vote on any large commercial project; the council adopted a 180-day pause while both were considered, the planning board then recommended a permanent ban across every zoning district, and councillors take those recommendations at a special meeting on 9 September. A week after the pause was adopted, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission accepted the company's preliminary permit application for filing and opened a window for comments, motions to intervene and competing applications, closing on 19 October. Both are on the page. The pair is worth holding together, because it is the clearest instance this record has of a small place saying no and a national regulator opening its own door on the same proposal at the same time.

Four entries have moved to what already came, and two of them came back with answers rather than silence, which is not the usual result. Austin's council voted unanimously on 27 August to start amending its Land Development Code for data centres, and amended the staff recommendation to require binding rules by the end of 2026, so the code that does not currently define the words data centre has a deadline against it. Elkhart's council voted nine to nothing the same night, after taking public comment, to stop accepting applications for data centres and battery energy storage through to 31 December 2027; the mayor said plainly that it is a pause and not a rejection. The two windows that shut are quieter. The European Commission's feedback period on the Cloud and AI Development Act closed on 27 August, and the Minnesota electric service agreement for the Google subsidiary at Hermantown closed to written comment on 28 August, with nothing published yet by either about what came in.

Three other entries are added. Platte County in Wyoming takes a recommendation to its commissioners at 1pm on 2 September to rezone 5,344 acres of ranch, agricultural and mining land to industrial, which is about twenty-two square kilometres, and the conditions attached to that recommendation are the interesting part: the land goes back to its old zoning if nothing is built in five years, and any development still needs a separate permit. Spokane County in Washington has published two hearings on different questions, the pause on 15 September and the zoning code that would replace it on 15 October, with speakers on the September date held to three minutes and to the pause alone. And the European Commission has a second window open on data sovereignty, closing on 8 September, asking what stops a European organisation reaching data held elsewhere and what risk comes from elsewhere reaching theirs; it is described as targeted at actors in the data value chain and does not say whether a person writing on their own account may take part, which is recorded here rather than smoothed over.

What the search checked and found shut, so it does not have to be searched again. Nothing new open on artificial intelligence has appeared in the United States Federal Register since 21 August. The European Commission's own consultation list carries nothing open on AI beyond the data sovereignty window above. The insurance and pensions authority EIOPA ran its consultation on AI governance and risk management in 2025 and closed it. Uruguay's national AI strategy consultation ran in 2024 and is shut, which is a different process from the children and digital environments consultation still open and still on this page. The United Kingdom has nothing new open with artificial intelligence in the title; a consultation on data intermediaries closes on 7 September but makes no reference to AI, so it is noted rather than listed. Two calls at the UN human rights office were read: the one on children and safety in digital spaces is on the page, and a call on technologies related to climate change closing 30 September is about climate technology rather than this subject, and is recorded here in case it is the right door for somebody else.

Five things found this run have a place but no usable date, and are held in the file rather than written onto the page. Martin County in Florida voted four to one on 25 August to put a possible one-year pause on the agenda of its next meeting and told staff to bring back a proposal, with no date set. Chesapeake in Virginia adopted a data centre permitting ordinance on 18 August and directed staff to run public listening sessions, which the city says are being finalised and will be posted when they are. Salem Township's court-ordered second planning commission meeting still has no date, and a second civil motion has now been filed there, over the posting of the supervisors' work session of 26 August and a proposal to restrict public comment to township residents and taxpayers. Spartanburg County's extra public safety committee meeting has no date. Fayetteville in North Carolina is to reconsider its rules in late October with no day set. Lincolnton, Bay City, Henry County in Virginia, Hall County in Georgia, Kokomo, Doylestown Township, Cumberland in Rhode Island, Minot, Northfield, Salem in Oregon, Grayslake, Cumberland County in North Carolina and Louisville are unchanged from last run and still without published days.

Nothing was dropped under the defence rule this run. Thirteen doors close within a fortnight of this scan: the UNESCO computing applications on 31 August; the Australian Senate inquiry into AI and data centres on 1 September; the Hendersonville hearing on 3 September; six on 8 September, being the United States time use survey questions, the European data sovereignty consultation, the second Minnesota agreement at Pine Island, and the hearings at Rockingham, LaGrange and Lubbock; the United Kingdom call for evidence on data regulation on 9 September; the Uruguay consultation and the Southport hearing on 10 September; and the Irish consultation on the Cloud and AI Development Act on 11 September. The soonest things on the page are the Pacific Islands Forum leaders meeting opening at Koror on 30 August, and on 31 August the first of Woodbury County's three town halls at Sloan and the closing of the UNESCO window.
