Ahead

What is coming, and where.

The other half of the watch. That page records what already happened; this one lists what has a date on it, so there is still time to do something about it. Soonest first. Every line carries a date, a place and a source.

How to read this

A door is different from a diary entry.

Most of what follows is a meeting somebody else is holding. You can watch it, read what comes out of it, and hold it to what it said. That is worth knowing, but it is not participation.

A few entries are marked Open door. Those are consultations, calls for evidence and comment periods: a published process where an ordinary person or a small organisation can put something in writing and have it counted. Those are the ones that matter most here, because Q9 asks who the many are and how they are actually asked, and a door nobody walked through is part of that answer.

Dates are given as the source states them. Where a source says a month but not a day, the entry says so rather than inventing precision. Defence and military events are left out under the same standing rule as the watch.

Texas House State Affairs Committee takes public testimony on data centres Open door Q10

Austin, Texas, United States

The committee meets to hear invited and public testimony on data centre development in Texas. The published charges lead with global competitiveness and national security, then cover the economic growth data centres bring, the training and education pathways for the jobs, the regulatory framework and whether it can be streamlined while communities still plan and manage growth, the implementation of Senate Bill 6, how grid-connected facilities might support grid resilience, and the planning process for proposed 765-kilovolt transmission lines. The notice explains how to register to testify and how to submit electronic comments, so a person can be counted without travelling to Austin. Water sits inside those charges rather than at the head of them, which is worth knowing before writing: the pressure that put this hearing on the calendar came from residents, including at Laredo on the Rio Grande, where the state neither meters data centre water use nor requires a permit to draw groundwater.

Mexico's regional forums on children, platforms and AI Q9

Nuevo León, then Chiapas, then Guerrero, Mexico

Three regional forums with parents, teachers and specialists, announced by President Claudia Sheinbaum: the north-east at Nuevo León on 19 August, the south and south-east at Chiapas on 3 September, and the south and centre at Guerrero on 17 September. Charlotte asks its residents what should replace the data centre pause Open door Q10

The city put a 150-day stop on new data centre approvals in June, running to 5 November, and before it lifts it the council has to propose changes, hold a public hearing and put new policy in place. The United States asks how to count AI use before it starts counting Open door Q9

United States, by email

The Bureau of Labor Statistics is taking comments on a proposed set of questions to add to the American Time Use Survey, asking people whether they use AI tools, what they use them for, and how that sits alongside work, study, housework and rest. The bureau says no federal survey currently links a person's AI use to a detailed record of their day. The questions are due to be asked from January 2027 for two years, so the comment period is the point at which the wording can still change. Comments go by email to the bureau's clearance officer.

Nine of the seventeen are doors, and two of them are new.

The soonest is Texas on 19 August, and it is closer to a door than this page recorded on 14 August: the House State Affairs Committee notice sets out how to register to testify and how to send electronic comments, which means a person in Laredo can be counted without driving to Austin. The other new one is Charlotte in North Carolina, where six sessions in September ask residents what should replace a data centre moratorium that expires on 5 November. Both were found by looking at local government rather than national, which is where the data centre argument is actually being had.

Spain now has two entries in different directions. The consultation on generative AI and performers closed on 14 August and has moved to what already came. The one that opened is broader and quieter: the 2026 update of the national Digital Decade roadmap, open until 18 September, which sets targets for AI take-up alongside connectivity and skills. It is the kind of door that closes without anyone outside government noticing it was open.

What was checked and found shut: France, where the citizen consultation on AI and digital priorities closed on 12 January and the one on digital sovereignty on 5 June; Brazil, where the data protection authority held its society consultation on the AI regulatory sandbox on 13 August with registrations having closed on 15 June, and where the transparency guide closed on 24 July and the ethical AI use guide on 4 May; Ireland, where the committee's pre-legislative scrutiny of the Regulation of Artificial Intelligence Bill 2026 closed on 13 April; the UN Global Dialogue on AI Governance, whose submission portal states that it has closed; and the Caribbean, where the first Caribbean Artificial Intelligence Forum was held on 23 and 24 July, before this page existed to say it was coming.

One search habit is worth passing on. A university calendar listing an Indigenous data sovereignty summit as an event is not the same as a date ahead: two of them still carry the April 2026 summit with no marking that it has passed. Every date on this page is read from a source that states the year and the day.

Five entries that earlier runs had held back for space are on the page again: the UNESCO ethics forum in Riyadh, the Pan African AI Summit in Accra, the ITU Plenipotentiary Conference in Doha, the Internet Governance Forum in Nairobi, and the UN Permanent Forum on Indigenous Issues in New York. A page has no shortage of space, and a dated meeting a person could go to belongs where people read, so nothing is held back for length from here on.

Entries move to what already came once the date passes. They are kept there with a note of what came of it where the watch logged an outcome, so an announcement can be checked against what actually happened. Every entry is also held one per line in a machine-readable file.
