The other half of the watch. That page records what already happened; this one lists what has a date on it, so there is still time to do something about it. Soonest first. Every line carries a date, a place and a source.

How to read this

A door is different from a diary entry.

Most of what follows is a meeting somebody else is holding. You can watch it, read what comes out of it, and hold it to what it said. That is worth knowing, but it is not participation.

A few entries are marked Open door. Those are consultations, calls for evidence and comment periods: a published process where an ordinary person or a small organisation can put something in writing and have it counted. Those are the ones that matter most here, because Q9 asks who the many are and how they are actually asked, and a door nobody walked through is part of that answer.

Dates are given as the source states them. Where a source says a month but not a day, the entry says so rather than inventing precision. Defence and military events are left out under the same standing rule as the watch.

Australia puts a draft privacy Bill out for comment, with artificial intelligence named in the reasons for it Open door Q9

Australia, through the Attorney-General's Department consultation portal

The Attorney-General's Department released a consultation paper and the Exposure Draft Privacy Amendment (Personal Data Protection) Bill 2026 on 31 August, and is taking submissions until Friday 18 September. There are around forty changes to the Privacy Act in it. Four are the ones a person outside the field can weigh without a law degree. A fair and reasonable test, so an organisation can no longer justify collecting more than it needs or using information for purposes people would never expect. A right to erasure, letting a person ask a large digital platform to destroy personal information it holds about them. A stronger standard for consent, aimed at hidden terms and take-it-or-leave-it conditions. And a stop on trading in personal information without clear permission, so shopping habits, online interests and location data are not bought and sold without the person knowing. The government's own reason for the timing puts new technology at the centre: it describes the reforms as meeting emerging risks from artificial intelligence and from wearable devices such as smart glasses. Who is invited is listed widely and individuals are named in it, alongside regulated entities, non-government organisations, industry, consumer organisations, legal experts, privacy advocates and academia, and the department asks for concise submissions of around a thousand words, which is a low bar to clear. One thing belongs with it because the department says it plainly on its own page: it may use AI tools to analyse the submissions it receives. This is the closest open door on the page to where the site is written, and the one that reaches furthest into an ordinary day.

It is free for those selected, paid for through the European Union's Global Gateway strategy, and there are 35 places. What it teaches is the UN Development Programme's human rights impact assessment method applied to artificial intelligence, digital public infrastructure and data-driven public services: scoping a case, identifying the risks and the effects on people, and writing mitigation and governance recommendations.
