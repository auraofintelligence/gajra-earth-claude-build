Asking

Three questions, and ways to ask them.

All of this comes down to one question asked properly and an answer written down in somebody's own words. That works between two people on a couch and it works at a table in a market. The three questions are right below, one for each word in the name. Under that is the range of ways to ask it, smallest first, so you can pick one that suits you rather than one that sounds impressive.

Three questions, one for each word

The association is called Joyful Responsible Abundance and there is one question aimed at each of the three. None of them use those words, which is a preference rather than a rule.

Asking somebody directly what joyful responsible abundance would mean to them works, and works well. People tend to pause, then think, and quite a few enjoy the sound of it. That is probably because the three words have not been worn smooth: they do not appear to be used together in sequence anywhere else, so there is no script to reach for and a person has to answer from scratch. If you want to ask it that way, ask it that way. Say all three though. Dropping one for being awkward in the mouth leaves the two-legged stool this project keeps warning about.

The questions below take the other road and ask about an ordinary week instead. Not because the phrase fails, but because a week produces specifics: the ferry, the power bill, the grandkids, the thing that would have to stop. Use whichever suits the person in front of you, or both.

Each one can be asked six ways, because who, what, where, when, why and how open different doors and people walk through different ones. The bolded question is the opener; the six under it are where to go if the conversation wants to keep going. Three values by six angles is eighteen questions and you will never need all of them.

Joyful

If things went well around here, and I mean actually well, what would be different in a normal week for you?

Six ways in, if the conversation wants more. Who would you see more of? · What would a good week look like? · Where would you spend time that you cannot now? · When in the year is it hardest? · Why does that one matter most? · How would you know things had actually got better? For a kid: what would make a really good day?

Responsible

And what would have to stop?

Six ways in. Who should answer for it when it goes wrong? · What would you refuse even if it made your own life easier? · Where is the line you would not let anyone cross? · When would you want to be asked again? · Why is that the limit? · How would you want to be able to object, and to whom?

Abundance

And who else would that be good for?

Six ways in. Who does it leave out? · What would be left over for anybody else? · Where does it stop reaching? · When does it run out, and would it hold in ten years? · Why is it worth handing on? · How would somebody else build their own with it?

Ask all three where you can. If a conversation only has room for one, ask the second: the bound is the part hardest to get anywhere else, and the part this project has least of.

Why these are alignment questions. Aiming a system at something requires knowing what the something is, and right now that gets specified by a small number of people: staff intuitions, preference data from paid raters, constitutional documents drafted by small teams, and engagement metrics standing in for what people want. Human values is the load-bearing phrase in the entire field, and there is no body of ordinary people, unprompted, describing what a good week contains and what they would refuse. These three collect exactly that.

The honest limits: a few hundred answers will not steer a model, they will contradict each other, which is question three, and nobody has shown how a line about the ferry turns into a training signal or a policy. That gap is real and unbridged. The bet is only that the input has to exist before anyone can argue about what to do with it, and at the moment it does not.

The range, smallest first

Send it to one person. A link and a sentence about why you thought of them. That is a real contribution and it is where most people will start, including most of the people who later do something bigger.

Ask one person you already know. Over a bench, in the car, in a group chat. Ask the three questions above, listen, and write down what they said. This is the whole practice at its smallest, and nothing about it is lesser than the table version.

Ask a few people over a week. The same three questions each time so the answers can be compared. No setup, no permission, no equipment.

Ask somewhere you already turn up. A club, a class, a shift, a congregation, a forum you post in. You are not building anything new; you are asking a room you are already standing in.

Set up a table. The biggest version, and a genuinely large ask: a public spot, strangers, hours of it. For plenty of people, including many who would be very good at the listening, it is not on at all, and anxiety being common and rising is a fact rather than an excuse. It is an option, not the entry point and not the standard.

People are built differently and that is the end of it. Pick the size that suits how you are actually wired, not the one that sounds most impressive. If you would rather stretch a little and see what happens, that is also a good reason to pick a bigger one, and the smaller rungs are still there on the days it is too much.

Everything after this applies at every size: the rule, the consent, the tool. Nobody has to build a stall to take part.

Whatever size you are running

You are not there to sign anybody up.

Help first

not in exchange

The help is not bait and it is not conditional. Fix the phone, explain the form, sit with the confusing letter. If they walk away without saying a word about abundance, the station still did its job that day.

Ask once

not a pitch

One question, asked plainly, then stop talking. A pitch teaches people to tell you what you want to hear, which poisons the only thing you came for.

Their words

not your summary

Write down what they actually said, not the tidy version. Nobody's answer is worth more than anybody else's, and that includes the ones that contradict this website: those get written down exactly the same way, not smoothed on the way in.

Before you write anything down

Say this out loud, in your own words, every time. It takes fifteen seconds and it is the difference between listening and harvesting.

Can I write that down? It would go on a public list of what people think a good life needs, with your name next to it, or without your name if you would rather. Either way you can have it taken off whenever you want, no questions, just tell me or email the address on the card.

If there is any hesitation at all, record it anonymously. A line with no name is worth the same on the record and costs the person nothing. Nobody's name goes down because they did not want to seem difficult.

The intake tool

Write down what you heard.

This works with no signal. Lines stay on this device, in this browser, and nowhere else: not on a server, because there is not one. When you are back in range, send the lot in one message. Clearing really clears.

    What to bring, if you are doing the table

    Only the biggest size needs any of this, and none of it is bought from anybody.

    The table. Somewhere people already are: a market, a library foyer, outside the shops, the hall after a meeting. Shade and a chair matter more than a banner. Two chairs on the same side of the table beats two chairs facing each other.

    The help. Whatever you are genuinely good at. Phones and accounts, government forms, printing, a letter that needs answering, wifi that will not connect, an email account nobody can get back into. Real problems, not demonstrations.

    Paper. Bring it even though the tool below exists. Phones die, signal drops, and some people will simply say more to a pen. The tool is for you afterwards, not for holding up between you and somebody.

    The three questions, written out. Ask them the same way each time so the answers can be compared. Reading them off a card is not awkward, it is honest.

    Nothing to sign. No clipboard of names, no sign-up sheet, no QR code that leads to a form. If someone wants to be on the record, that happens later, deliberately, on the sign-on page.

    What happens to what you heard

    Each line becomes one row in the signatories file, in the same column where every other definition sits, credited to the person or marked anonymous exactly as they asked. That is the whole point of the column existing: the three words are supposed to be defined by the people who have to live inside them, not by whoever built the website.

    If the person wanted their place shown, the same pull request adds it to the map, at town or island scale. If a station runs more than once, the station itself can go on the map, so the next person can find you rather than starting their own from scratch.

    And if what you heard argues with something on this site, say so in the message. The open questions exist because the answers are not settled, and a station that only ever confirms what the site already says has stopped being a listening station.
